# What are smartphones used for besides calls and messages? #card

Smartphones are used for taking high-quality photos and videos, accessing social media platforms, sending emails, managing bank accounts, using GPS navigation, playing games, shopping online, making electronic payments, attending online classes, storing documents, scanning QR codes, and controlling smart home devices such as lights, cameras, and thermostats.

# Main benefits of smartphones in society? #card

Smartphones improve communication by allowing instant contact worldwide, provide fast access to information and learning resources, support online education, enable digital government and banking services, help people manage work efficiently, increase business opportunities, and connect communities through social networks.

# Main problems of smartphone use? #card

Smartphones create serious privacy risks through data tracking, cause distraction while driving and studying, increase addiction and screen dependency, reduce face-to-face interaction, contribute to anxiety and sleep problems, enable cyberbullying, and allow constant surveillance by companies and governments.

# Why is location tracking dangerous? #card

Location tracking allows companies, hackers, criminals, or governments to monitor users’ movements, habits, and routines, which can lead to stalking, robbery planning, discrimination, political profiling, or misuse of personal information without user consent.

# Why are phone cameras a privacy issue? #card

Phone cameras allow secret recording in public and private spaces such as schools, changing rooms, or homes without permission, which can violate dignity, spread embarrassing content online, enable blackmail, and permanently damage someone’s reputation.

# What is digital rudeness (phone etiquette)? #card

Digital rudeness means using phones during conversations, classes, prayers, meetings, or family time, ignoring people physically present, playing loud media, checking messages constantly, and showing disrespect by prioritizing devices over human interaction.

# What is sexting and why is it risky? #card

Sexting is sending sexual images, videos, or messages through digital devices. It is risky because content can be shared without permission, used for blackmail, cause emotional harm, lead to cyberbullying, and result in serious legal consequences, especially for minors.

# What is a kill switch? #card

A kill switch is a technical feature that allows companies or authorities to remotely disable software, block accounts, delete files, erase devices, or shut down services without physical access to the user’s device.

# Main purpose of kill switches? #card

Kill switches are mainly used to remove malware, disable stolen phones, stop dangerous software, block illegal content, protect networks from attacks, and reduce the spread of harmful applications.

# Why are kill switches ethically problematic? #card

Kill switches reduce real ownership, limit user control, threaten privacy, allow hidden surveillance, lack transparency, and can be abused by companies or governments to censor content or control users without proper explanation.

# What happened in the Amazon Kindle case? #card

Amazon remotely deleted copyrighted books from users’ Kindle devices after discovering licensing problems, showing that customers did not truly own their digital purchases and that companies could control purchased content.

# What did Google’s Android kill switch prove? #card

Google demonstrated that it can remotely remove applications from millions of Android devices, proving that large companies have centralized control over users’ software without direct user approval.

# Main ethical issue in kill switch cases? #card

The main ethical issue is that users are rarely informed clearly about this power, cannot refuse it easily, and have limited legal protection against misuse or mistakes.

# What is social networking? #card

Social networking refers to online platforms such as Facebook, Instagram, X, and TikTok that allow users to create profiles, share content, communicate, form communities, and build professional or personal relationships.

# Benefits of social networking? #card

Social networking improves communication, supports social movements, helps small businesses advertise, enables fundraising, spreads awareness, allows global collaboration, and gives individuals a voice in public discussions.

# Main dangers of social networking? #card

Main dangers include cyberbullying, online harassment, stalking, misinformation, fake news, addiction, loss of privacy, mental health problems, identity theft, and manipulation by political or commercial groups.

# What is a socialbot? #card

A socialbot is an automated AI-controlled account that pretends to be human, posts messages, interacts with users, and influences opinions on social media platforms.

# Why are socialbots dangerous? #card

They spread propaganda, promote scams, distort public opinion, amplify false information, manipulate elections, and reduce trust in online communication.

# Meaning of “On the Internet, nobody knows you’re a dog”? #card

This phrase means that online users can easily hide their real identity, age, gender, nationality, or intentions, making it difficult to know who you are really communicating with.

# What is privacy? #card

Privacy is the fundamental right of individuals to control their personal information, protect their personal space, make private decisions, and live without unnecessary observation or interference.

# Three main meanings of privacy? #card

Privacy includes freedom from physical and digital intrusion, control over personal data, and freedom from constant tracking or profiling by organizations or governments.

# Why is online privacy fragile? #card

Online privacy is fragile because data is stored permanently, copied instantly, shared globally, sold commercially, hacked easily, and often reused without user knowledge.

# What is personal information? #card

Personal information is any data that identifies or describes a person, such as name, ID number, address, phone number, email, IP address, location history, photos, biometric data, or usernames.

# What is informed consent? #card

Informed consent means users agree to data collection only after receiving clear, understandable, and complete information about how their data will be used, stored, and shared.

# Why is informed consent often weak? #card

It is weak because privacy policies are long, technical, difficult to understand, rarely read, frequently changed, and often presented as “accept or leave” options.

# What is invisible data collection? #card

Invisible data collection is the gathering of personal data without users noticing it, such as background tracking, hidden scripts, or automatic logging.

# Example of invisible data collection? #card

Examples include tracking browsing behavior with hidden cookies, recording app usage, monitoring location in the background, and collecting contact lists without clear permission.

# What are cookies? #card

Cookies are small text files stored on a user’s device by websites to remember login details, preferences, language settings, shopping carts, and browsing behavior, helping sites function properly and personalize services.

# Benefits of cookies? #card

Cookies improve user experience by saving passwords, remembering settings, speeding up page loading, enabling online shopping carts, and providing personalized recommendations and relevant advertisements.

# Risks of cookies? #card

Cookies can track users across multiple websites, build detailed behavior profiles, invade privacy, share data with third parties, and be misused for surveillance without clear consent.

# What are supercookies? #card

Supercookies are advanced tracking technologies stored outside normal browser storage that can recreate deleted cookies, making it difficult for users to avoid monitoring.

# What is secondary use? #card

Secondary use is when collected personal data is later used for advertising, profiling, political targeting, or selling to third parties without the user’s original permission.

# What is data mining? #card

Data mining is the process of analyzing large databases to discover hidden patterns, trends, correlations, and user behaviors for business, research, or surveillance purposes.

# What is profiling? #card

Profiling is creating detailed digital models of users’ interests, habits, personality, income level, beliefs, and risks based on collected data.

# Difference: opt-in vs opt-out? #card

Opt-in requires users to actively give permission before data is collected, while opt-out collects data automatically unless users take steps to refuse later.

# Why is opt-in more ethical? #card

Opt-in respects user autonomy, promotes transparency, prevents manipulation, and ensures that consent is meaningful rather than forced.

# What is spam? #card

Spam is unwanted mass electronic messages sent through email, SMS, or social media, usually for advertising, scams, malware distribution, or fraud.

# Why is spam harmful? #card

Spam wastes time, fills inboxes, spreads viruses, enables identity theft, overloads servers, reduces productivity, and tricks vulnerable users.

# Do spam filters violate free speech? #card

Spam filters do not violate free speech because users voluntarily choose to block unwanted messages and no one has the right to force others to receive content.

# What is phishing? #card

Phishing is a cybercrime technique where attackers send fake messages pretending to be banks, companies, or authorities to steal passwords, credit cards, or personal data.

# What is smishing? #card

Smishing is phishing conducted through SMS messages that contain fake links, urgent warnings, or prize announcements to trick users.

# What is vishing? #card

Vishing is phishing through phone calls where criminals pretend to be officials, support agents, or relatives to obtain sensitive information.

# What is pharming? #card

Pharming redirects users from legitimate websites to fake ones by manipulating DNS systems or devices, even when the correct address is typed.

# Why is identity theft dangerous? #card

Identity theft can lead to financial losses, ruined credit scores, false criminal records, emotional stress, legal problems, and long-term reputation damage.

# What is hacking? #card

Hacking is intentional and unauthorized access to computer systems, networks, or data by bypassing security measures to steal, manipulate, or disrupt information.

# Phase 1 meaning of hacking (1960s–70s)? #card

In this period, hacking meant creative problem-solving, improving programs, sharing knowledge, and exploring systems for learning purposes.

# Phase 2 meaning of hacking (1970s–1990s)? #card

Hacking became linked to illegal activities such as breaking into networks, stealing passwords, spreading viruses, and exploiting security weaknesses.

# Phase 3 meaning of hacking (1990s–present)? #card

Hacking evolved into organized cybercrime, including identity theft, ransomware, corporate espionage, political attacks, and global hacking groups.

# What is hacktivism? #card

Hacktivism is the use of hacking methods to protest governments, corporations, or policies by leaking data, defacing websites, or blocking services.

# What is a denial-of-service attack (DoS)? #card

A DoS attack floods a website or server with massive traffic, making it slow or completely unavailable to legitimate users.

# What are zombie computers? #card

Zombie computers are infected devices secretly controlled by hackers and used together in botnets to launch attacks or send spam.

# Why is “harmless hacking” unethical? #card

Even without bad intentions, it violates privacy, breaks trust, consumes resources, exposes vulnerabilities, and can cause accidental harm.

# Example of harm from non-malicious hacking? #card

A student testing security may crash a system, leak data, force expensive audits, and damage the organization’s reputation.

# Ethical view of hacking? #card

Ethically, hacking is digital trespassing that ignores consent, threatens safety, and undermines trust in technology systems.

# What is malware? #card

Malware is malicious software designed to damage systems, steal data, spy on users, or take control of devices.

# What is a computer virus? #card

A virus attaches itself to legitimate programs and spreads when files are shared, often damaging or corrupting data.

# What is a worm? #card

A worm spreads automatically through networks without user action, rapidly infecting many systems.

# What is spyware? #card

Spyware secretly monitors user activity, records keystrokes, tracks browsing, and sends data to attackers.

# What is Artificial Intelligence (AI)? #card

AI refers to computer systems that simulate human abilities such as learning, reasoning, recognizing speech, making decisions, and solving problems.

# What is the Turing Test? #card

The Turing Test evaluates whether a machine can communicate in a way that is indistinguishable from a human.

# When does a machine pass the Turing Test? #card

It passes when human judges cannot reliably tell whether they are talking to a machine or a person.

# Main idea behind the Turing Test? #card

It focuses on observable behavior rather than internal mental states to define intelligence.

# Who criticized the Turing Test? #card

Philosopher John Searle criticized it for confusing imitation with real understanding.

# What is the Chinese Room argument? #card

It argues that computers manipulate symbols using rules without understanding their meaning.

# What does Searle claim about AI? #card

He claims machines simulate thinking but lack consciousness, awareness, and true comprehension.

# Why is IBM Watson not “intelligent” for Searle? #card

Watson processes data statistically but does not understand concepts like humans do.

# Main ethical concern about AI? #card

AI raises ethical concerns about massive job replacement, biased decision-making, lack of accountability when systems fail, invasion of privacy, misuse for surveillance or warfare, and the risk of humans losing control over critical technologies.

# What is algorithmic bias? #card

Algorithmic bias occurs when AI systems produce unfair or discriminatory results because they are trained on biased, incomplete, or unrepresentative data.

# Why is transparency important in AI? #card

Transparency allows users and regulators to understand how decisions are made, detect errors or discrimination, build trust, and hold developers accountable.

# What is automation bias? #card

Automation bias is the tendency of people to blindly trust computer systems and follow their recommendations even when they are wrong.

# Software Engineering Code: Public principle #card

Engineers must prioritize public safety, human rights, environmental protection, and social welfare over profit or personal interests.

# Software Engineering Code: Client principle #card

Engineers should serve clients honestly while refusing projects that harm society, violate laws, or endanger users.

# Software Engineering Code: Product principle #card

Engineers must ensure that software is reliable, secure, tested, documented, and free from dangerous defects.

# Software Engineering Code: Judgment principle #card

Engineers must make professional decisions based on evidence, ethics, and honesty, without corruption or pressure.

# Software Engineering Code: Management principle #card

Managers must promote ethical practices, fair treatment, quality assurance, and responsible project planning.

# Software Engineering Code: Profession principle #card

Engineers should enhance the reputation of their profession by following standards and reporting unethical behavior.

# Software Engineering Code: Colleagues principle #card

Engineers should respect coworkers, share knowledge, avoid sabotage, and support professional growth.

# Software Engineering Code: Self principle #card

Engineers must continually update their skills, reflect on ethical responsibilities, and act with integrity.

# ACM principle: Contribute to society #card

Computing professionals should design systems that improve education, health, safety, accessibility, and quality of life.

# ACM principle: Avoid harm #card

Professionals must minimize physical, psychological, financial, and social damage caused by technology.

# ACM principle: Be honest #card

They must communicate limitations, risks, errors, and conflicts of interest truthfully.

# ACM principle: Avoid discrimination #card

They must ensure technologies do not unfairly disadvantage groups based on race, gender, religion, or disability.

# ACM principle: Honor property #card

Professionals must respect intellectual property rights, licenses, and ownership laws.

# ACM principle: Give credit #card

They should properly acknowledge contributors, sources, and previous work.

# ACM principle: Respect privacy #card

They must protect personal data and design systems with privacy safeguards.

# ACM principle: Honor confidentiality #card

They must keep sensitive information secure unless disclosure is legally required.

# What is intellectual property (IP)? #card

Intellectual property refers to creations of the mind such as inventions, software, books, designs, and trademarks that are legally protected.

# Why is IP important? #card

IP encourages innovation by rewarding creators, supporting economic growth, and protecting investments in research.

# What does copyright protect? #card

Copyright protects original literary, artistic, musical, and digital works from unauthorized copying or distribution.

# What is not copyrightable? #card

Facts, ideas, mathematical formulas, methods, and natural discoveries cannot be copyrighted.

# What is a patent? #card

A patent grants inventors exclusive rights to produce and sell their invention for a limited time.

# Patent requirements? #card

An invention must be new, useful, non-obvious, and fully described.

# Patent duration in Kosovo? #card

Patents are valid for 20 years if maintenance fees are paid.

# What is a trademark? #card

A trademark protects logos, brand names, slogans, and symbols used to identify products.

# Purpose of trademarks? #card

They help consumers recognize genuine products and prevent brand imitation.

# What is a trade secret? #card

Trade secrets are confidential business information that provides competitive advantage.

# Example of trade secret? #card

The Coca-Cola formula is a famous protected trade secret.

# What do you buy when buying a book? #card

You buy the physical or digital copy, not the right to reproduce or distribute it.

# What is fair use? #card

Fair use allows limited use of copyrighted material for education, criticism, research, or news reporting.

# Purpose of fair use? #card

It balances creator rights with public access to knowledge.

# Fair use factor 1? #card

Purpose: whether use is educational, nonprofit, or transformative.

# Fair use factor 2? #card

Nature: whether the work is factual or creative.

# Fair use factor 3? #card

Amount: how much of the original work is used.

# Fair use factor 4? #card

Market effect: impact on the creator’s income.

# Why is illegal copying unethical? #card

Illegal copying is unethical because it deprives creators of income, discourages creativity, violates legal agreements, harms creative industries, and unfairly benefits users who do not pay for others’ work.

# Common excuse: “I can’t afford it” #card

Not being able to afford content does not justify stealing, because creators depend on sales for their livelihood and free legal alternatives often exist.

# Sony vs Universal (1984) ruling #card

The court ruled that recording TV programs for personal home use (time-shifting) is legal and considered fair use.

# MGM v Grokster ruling #card

The court decided that companies promoting piracy tools are responsible for copyright infringement by users.

# What is DRM? #card

Digital Rights Management is technology that restricts copying, sharing, printing, or modifying digital content.

# Purpose of DRM? #card

DRM protects copyright holders by preventing unauthorized distribution and revenue loss.

# Main criticism of DRM? #card

DRM limits legitimate user rights, restricts backups, reduces interoperability, and may lock users into specific platforms.

# What is DMCA? #card

The Digital Millennium Copyright Act is a US law that protects copyrighted works and bans breaking DRM systems.

# DMCA anti-circumvention rule #card

It makes it illegal to bypass copy-protection technologies, even for personal use in many cases.

# DMCA safe harbor #card

It protects online platforms from liability if they remove illegal content when notified.

# What is jailbreaking? #card

Jailbreaking removes manufacturer restrictions to allow custom apps or system changes.

# What is Creative Commons? #card

Creative Commons provides licenses that let creators share work under flexible conditions.

# Main CC benefit? #card

It encourages legal sharing, collaboration, and educational use.

# What is GNU GPL? #card

GNU General Public License allows free use and modification but requires sharing improvements.

# Meaning of copyleft? #card

Copyleft ensures that modified versions remain free for everyone.

# What is telecommuting? #card

Telecommuting is working from home using digital communication tools.

# Main benefit of telecommuting? #card

It saves time, reduces stress, improves work-life balance, and lowers costs.

# Productivity effect of telecommuting? #card

Many workers are more productive due to fewer interruptions.

# Environmental benefit? #card

Less commuting reduces traffic congestion and pollution.

# Financial benefit for workers? #card

Workers save money on fuel, transport, food, and clothing.

# Main problem of telecommuting? #card

It can cause loneliness, weak teamwork, and burnout.

# Loyalty issue in telecommuting? #card

Remote workers may feel less connected to company culture.

# Security risk in telecommuting? #card

Home networks are often less secure, increasing hacking risks.

# What is outsourcing? #card

Outsourcing means hiring external companies for services.

# What is offshoring? #card

Offshoring moves jobs to other countries.

# What is inshoring? #card

Inshoring brings foreign companies to hire local workers.

# Why do companies offshore? #card

To reduce costs, access skilled labor, and operate 24/7.

# Example of offshoring? #card

Customer support centers in India.

# Main complaint about offshoring? #card

Language barriers and time differences reduce service quality.

# How does tech destroy jobs? #card

Machines replace repetitive and manual work.

# How does tech create jobs? #card

It creates new fields like cybersecurity and AI.

# Example of job loss from tech? #card

# Example of job creation from tech? #card

Online platforms and cloud services.

# Why is job transition difficult? #card

Workers need new skills and training.

# Role of education in job change? #card

Education helps workers adapt.

# Why do people fear technology? #card

They worry about unemployment and inequality.

# What is workforce adaptation? #card

Learning modern skills continuously.

# Long-term effect of technology? #card

Higher productivity and economic growth.
